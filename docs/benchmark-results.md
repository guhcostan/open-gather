## Method (scenario A and E)

- Bots join over HTTP, connect a WebSocket and walk BFS paths between random targets, spending ~30 % of the time walking, like people who stop to talk.
- *distributed*: targets anywhere on the 60x36-tile starter map (interest areas still cover most of it); *concentrated*: everybody inside the 17x13-tile reception, the worst case for fan-out.
- Propagation latency: probe bots change direction and another bot in the same process stamps when the server's frame shows the change (one clock, includes the generator's own scheduling).
- Tick percentiles come from a coarse histogram (upper bucket bound); mean and max are exact.

## Reading the numbers

- **Bandwidth was the quadratic cost.** 1,000 concentrated bots wrote 58 MB/s (about 465 Mbit/s) before and 11.5 MB/s (about 92 Mbit/s) after. On a hosting plan with an egress quota or a 100 Mbit/s port this is the number that matters, not CPU.
- **Tick budget:** at 15 Hz a tick has 66.7 ms. Before the change the worst tick of the 1,000 concentrated run (142.9 ms) exceeded it; after, the worst tick was 59.8 ms (mean 6.7 ms). That is *inside* the budget but with a thin margin on this laptop; a slower CPU would not have it.
- **Latency did not move:** median 30-46 ms, p95 about 64-67 ms, p99 about 68-82 ms on loopback. It is dominated by the 15 Hz flush (a state change waits for the next tick), so a network round trip adds on top. The 150 ms target has **not** been validated across a real network.
- **Memory:** server RSS 127 MB with 1,000 connected bots (Go heap about 34 MB at the end of the run); no server-side growth was concluded from these short runs.
- **Server CPU** (percent of one core, sampled with §ps§) was 45-54 % at 1,000 bots, but the generator was competing for the same cores, so this is an upper bound for the server and not a sizing figure.
- **Mass reconnect (scenario E):** 500 clients dropped at once and reconnected (with up to 2 s of client-side jitter, as the web app does) in 2.0 s, 0 failures, 0 kicked clients.
- **Proximity grouping cost:** 300 concentrated bots with media consent formed 64 groups; mean tick 2.15 ms, the same order as the same run without groups.

## Media through the SFU (scenarios B, C, D)

Synthetic but **real** media: [bench/mediagen](../bench/mediagen) publishes an Opus track (~56 kbit/s on the wire) and a 640x360 VP8 track (~520 kbit/s) from encoded files, and every participant subscribes to the others through a local LiveKit server 1.13.7 and measures the RTP it actually receives (loss from sequence gaps, RFC 3550 jitter, stalls over 500 ms). Everything ran on the same M1 Pro laptop; **the SFU CPU is percent of one core** (100 = one core), sampled with `ps`, and the generator competes for the same CPU. - **B** = 5 calls of 4 (20 of the 100 present are in calls; the other 80 cost the app server only, see scenario A). **C-10x4** and **C-15x4** are *scaled-down* versions of scenario C (40 and 60 people instead of 100); **C-25x4-audioonly** is 100 people with audio only. **D** = one meeting of 20, each receiving at most 6 camera videos, one 720p screen share.
- **Valid results are the ones with 0 % loss:** B (20 people), C-10x4 (40 people) and D (20 people). The SFU used 46 %, 123 % and 152 % of a core while forwarding 35, 69 and 107 Mbit/s: **roughly 1.3-1.8 % of one M1 Pro core per Mbit/s forwarded**, in these three points.
- **C-15x4 (60 people) already lost 34 % of the packets** and the audio-only run with 100 people lost 61 % and could not connect 17 participants. With the generator on the same laptop we cannot tell how much of that is the SFU and how much the 100+ WebRTC clients (and their DTLS/SRTP work) fighting for the same cores and the loopback socket buffers. **Scenario C at its full size (100 people in 25 calls of 4) was attempted and failed** (first attempt with video: only 48 of 100 connected, 74 % loss; raw JSON kept in [bench/results/media-invalid-full-c](../bench/results/media-invalid-full-c/)). It needs a generator on another machine.
- **ESTIMATE, not measured:** if one vCPU of the 2 vCPU reference server were 2-3x slower than an M1 Pro core (an assumption nobody has verified here), the same ratio would mean ~3-5 % of a vCPU per Mbit/s forwarded, i.e. roughly 40-65 Mbit/s (80-120 forwarded 500 kbit/s video streams) before the SFU alone uses both vCPUs. Treat this as a hypothesis to test on the real server.
- Not measured: TURN-relayed media, simulcast layer switching under congestion (the test tracks are single-layer, the browser client publishes two layers), a long run, packet loss injected on the network.

What it shows:

- **Presence (no media).** Every bot joined in every run and nobody was kicked. 100 bots was the last run where every tick percentile fit the histogram (p99 5 ms, max 82 ms, against a 66 ms tick period at 15 Hz). At 200 bots the mean tick was still 3.1 ms and the median move reached others in 88 ms, but p99 went above 50 ms, the worst tick took 180 ms and p95 latency rose to 230 ms. From 300 bots the busy share stopped growing at about 22 % while steal passed 70 %, consistent with the shape's CPU cap; at 500 the mean tick (99 ms) exceeded the tick period, so the server was saturated even though nothing failed. Caddy, terminating TLS for every WebSocket, used 36 to 57 % of the CPU the app and Caddy used together.
- **Media.** One meeting of 4 people with video and 20 people in audio-only groups of 4 ran with no loss that matters (0 % and 0.5 %). Twelve people with video lost 27 %, twenty lost 63 %, and the 60-person audio-only run (59 connected) lost 51 % at only 4.8 Mbit/s. Steal above 60 % in the worst runs makes the VM's CPU cap the most likely limit, but the generator laptop's CPU and home upload were not measured, so the generator side is not ruled out. These runs say nothing about a VM with dedicated CPUs.
- **Reading it (estimate, not a capacity claim).** On a free micro VM, about 100 people walking around stayed smooth and 200 still worked with slower worst cases; one small video call ran clean and three did not. Single runs, one region, one generator machine.
